A joint is more than a meeting of bones - it is a mechanical system that translates muscle force into controlled motion. The same biomechanical principles - **levers, angles of pull, range of motion, stability vs mobility tradeoffs** - explain why the shoulder dislocates often, why the hip almost never does, why we can lift a heavy box close to the body but not at arm's length, and why ACL tears are so common in cutting sports.

## Joint classification (functional)

| Category | Movement | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Synarthrosis** (fibrous, immovable) | None | Skull sutures, tooth-socket gomphoses |
| **Amphiarthrosis** (cartilaginous, slightly movable) | Limited | Pubic symphysis, intervertebral discs, manubriosternal joint |
| **Diarthrosis** (synovial, freely movable) | Full | Most joints of limbs - shoulder, hip, knee, elbow, wrist |

Synovial joints are the focus of biomechanics. They have a **capsule** lined with **synovium**, **articular cartilage** (hyaline) on the bone ends, a **joint cavity** filled with **synovial fluid** (hyaluronic acid → lubrication + shock absorption), and **stabilizing ligaments** and surrounding **muscle/tendon** units.

## Six types of synovial joints

<Figure name="skeletal/synovial-joint" caption="Synovial joint - capsule lined by synovium, joint cavity filled with synovial fluid, articular (hyaline) cartilage on bone ends, supporting ligaments." />

<Figure name="skeletal/synovial-joint-types" caption="The six types of synovial joints - hinge (elbow, knee), pivot (atlantoaxial), plane (intercarpal), saddle (1st CMC), condyloid (wrist, MCP), ball-and-socket (shoulder, hip)." />

| Type | Shape | Axes of motion | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hinge** | Convex-concave cylinder | 1 (flexion/extension) | Elbow (humeroulnar), knee, IP joints, ankle |
| **Pivot** | Ring around peg | 1 (rotation) | Atlantoaxial (C1-C2), proximal radioulnar |
| **Plane (gliding)** | Flat surfaces | Small translation in any direction | Intercarpal, intertarsal, facet (zygapophyseal) joints |
| **Saddle** | Concave-convex saddle | 2 (flex/ext + abd/add) | First carpometacarpal (thumb) - allows opposition |
| **Condyloid (ellipsoidal)** | Oval condyle in oval socket | 2 (flex/ext + abd/add, no rotation) | Radiocarpal (wrist), MCPs |
| **Ball and socket** | Spherical head in cup | 3 (all) | Shoulder (glenohumeral), hip |

**Mobility vs stability** trade-off: ball-and-socket joints are highly mobile but unstable (shoulder); hinge joints are very stable but only move one axis (elbow).

## Movements at synovial joints - terminology

<Figure name="skeletal/synovial-joint-types" caption="Six synovial joint types - plane, hinge, pivot, condyloid, saddle, ball-and-socket. Different shapes permit different movement axes." />

| Movement | Description |
|---|---|
| **Flexion** | Decrease in joint angle in sagittal plane |
| **Extension** | Increase in joint angle in sagittal plane |
| **Abduction** | Movement away from midline in frontal plane |
| **Adduction** | Movement toward midline |
| **Rotation** (medial/lateral) | Around long axis |
| **Circumduction** | Combination of flex/ext + abd/add → cone shape |
| **Pronation** | Forearm: palm faces posteriorly (radius crosses ulna) |
| **Supination** | Forearm: palm faces anteriorly |
| **Inversion** | Sole turns medially (subtalar joint) |
| **Eversion** | Sole turns laterally |
| **Dorsiflexion** | Ankle: foot up |
| **Plantarflexion** | Ankle: toes pointed |
| **Elevation / Depression** | Scapula or mandible up/down |
| **Protraction / Retraction** | Scapula or mandible forward/back |
| **Opposition / Reposition** | Thumb to fingers / return |

## Levers - the mechanical basis of muscle action

<Figure name="skeletal/long-bone" caption="Related figure for context — Levers - the mechanical basis of muscle action." />

Every muscle-bone-joint system is a **lever**. A lever has:
- **Fulcrum** - the joint
- **Effort** - the muscle force
- **Load** - the resistance (limb weight + external object)
- **Effort arm** - distance from fulcrum to effort
- **Load arm** - distance from fulcrum to load
- **Mechanical advantage** = effort arm / load arm

| Class | Arrangement | Mechanical advantage | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First class** | Effort - Fulcrum - Load | Variable | Atlanto-occipital joint - posterior neck muscles (effort) extend the head (load) about the joint (fulcrum) |
| **Second class** | Fulcrum - Load - Effort | > 1 (always) | Standing on tiptoes - ball of foot (fulcrum), body weight (load), gastrocnemius via Achilles (effort) |
| **Third class** | Fulcrum - Effort - Load | < 1 (always) | Most body levers - biceps flexing elbow (fulcrum is elbow, effort biceps insertion is between, load is forearm + hand) |

**Almost all human levers are third-class** - sacrificing force for **speed and range of motion**. A small contraction at the biceps moves the hand a long way fast. The price: the biceps must generate ~6× the weight in the hand because its insertion is so close to the fulcrum.

<Note title="Why carrying a heavy box close to the body is easier">
The load arm is the distance from the joint (elbow or shoulder) to the box's center of mass. Hold a 5 kg box at the elbow: short load arm → biceps needs to generate ~30 kg of pull. Hold the same box at arm's length: load arm is ~3× longer → biceps must pull ~90 kg. Same weight, three times the muscle force. Ergonomic lifting rule: **keep the load close**.
</Note>

## Length-tension relationship

A muscle generates maximum force when sarcomeres are at **optimal length** (~2.0-2.2 μm) - all myosin heads can engage actin without overlap.

- **Too short** (overlap of thin filaments) - reduced force
- **Too long** (no thick-thin overlap) - reduced force
- **Optimal** - max active tension

Joint angle determines sarcomere length. Biceps generates peak force at ~90° elbow flexion. This is why curl strength feels weak at the bottom and at the very top.

## Force-velocity relationship

- Concentric (shortening) - faster shortening → less force
- Eccentric (lengthening, "negative") - generates the *most* force; also the most muscle damage and DOMS
- Isometric - constant length, no movement

Strength training uses this: 1-rep max (slow) generates more force than 10-rep max (faster).

## Joint stability - five sources

1. **Bony congruence** - hip (deep acetabulum) is highly stable; shoulder (shallow glenoid) is not.
2. **Ligaments** - passive restraints. Cut a ligament, joint dislocates.
3. **Capsule** - reinforced by thickened bands.
4. **Muscle and tendon** - dynamic stabilizers. Rotator cuff for the shoulder is paramount.
5. **Negative intra-articular pressure** - suction effect, especially in hip; relieved during anesthesia.

## Clinical biomechanics - common injuries

<Figure name="skeletal/knee-frontal" caption="Knee anatomy as the model 3rd-class lever - quadriceps insertion on the tibial tuberosity, patella as a sesamoid that increases the moment arm by ~50% (Servier Medical Art)." />

<Figure name="skeletal/ankle-sprain-servier" caption="Ankle inversion sprain - lateral ligaments torn (ATFL first); the lever arm of the foot makes this the most common sports injury." />

<Clinical>
**Shoulder dislocation** - **anterior >> posterior >> inferior**. Anterior dislocations (95%) follow forced abduction + external rotation + extension (the "tackle" or "fall on outstretched hand"). The humeral head pops out of the glenoid anteroinferiorly. Axillary nerve at risk → check deltoid sensation and contraction.

**Rotator cuff tear** - most often supraspinatus (passes under the acromion - impingement zone). Painful arc 60-120° abduction.

**Knee - ACL tear** - non-contact pivot injury (cutting sports). Anterior tibial translation, positive Lachman, audible pop. Female athletes have 4-8× higher rate (anatomic + neuromuscular factors).

**Knee - meniscal tear** - twisting with weight bearing. Joint-line tenderness, locking. McMurray test.

**Hip - femoral neck fracture** in elderly osteoporotic patients - low-energy fall → shortened externally-rotated leg. Risk of avascular necrosis of femoral head (medial femoral circumflex artery damaged).

**Ankle sprain** - inversion injury → anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) most commonly torn. RICE + functional rehab.

**Osteoarthritis** - "wear-and-tear" cartilage loss. Joint space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis + cysts on X-ray. Weight-bearing joints (knee, hip), DIP > PIP > MCP. Treat with weight loss, exercise, NSAIDs, intra-articular steroids, eventual replacement.

**Rheumatoid arthritis** - autoimmune synovitis. Symmetric small-joint involvement (MCP, PIP, wrist, MTP) sparing DIP. Morning stiffness > 1 h. Pannus erodes cartilage and bone. Treat with DMARDs (methotrexate first-line); biologics for refractory.
</Clinical>

## Proprioception - your sixth sense

<Figure name="skeletal/synovial-joint" caption="Related figure for context — Proprioception - your sixth sense." />

Joint position and movement are sensed by:
- **Muscle spindles** (Ia and II afferents) - sense muscle length and velocity
- **Golgi tendon organs** (Ib afferents) - sense muscle tension
- **Joint capsule mechanoreceptors** (Ruffini, Pacinian-like, free nerve endings)
- **Cutaneous mechanoreceptors** - skin stretch around joints

Without proprioception (lost in tabes dorsalis, B12 deficiency, large-fiber neuropathy), you cannot walk in the dark - Romberg sign positive. Proprioception is essential for **coordinated movement** and is heavily integrated with cerebellar feedback.
