The adult skeleton has **206 bones** organized into the **axial skeleton** (skull, vertebral column, thoracic cage - 80 bones) and the **appendicular skeleton** (upper and lower limbs plus their girdles - 126 bones). Bones serve five jobs: support, protection, movement, mineral storage (calcium, phosphate), and hematopoiesis (red marrow).

## Two skeletons, two jobs

The slides stress that the skeleton is not one uniform frame:

- The **axial skeleton** is the central protective column: skull, vertebral column, ribs, and sternum.
- The **appendicular skeleton** is built for leverage and locomotion: limbs plus their girdles.
- The **pectoral girdle** (clavicle + scapula) prioritizes mobility.
- The **pelvic girdle** prioritizes load transfer and stability.

## Bone is not the only skeletal tissue

The lecture deck also treats **cartilage** as part of the skeletal system because important load-bearing regions stay cartilaginous:

- **Costal cartilage** gives the thoracic cage flexibility during breathing.
- **Intervertebral discs** act as fibrocartilaginous shock absorbers.
- The **pubic symphysis** is a secondary cartilaginous joint that sacrifices motion for stability.

That distinction matters clinically: **bone fractures heal with mineralized callus**, but **cartilage injuries heal slowly** because cartilage is avascular.

## Radiograph anchors

<Figure name="skeletal/radiographs-slide" caption="Lecture slide using plain radiographs to reinforce a few skeletal landmarks that students are expected to recognize quickly in clinic and on exams." />

The MSK slides do not stop at idealized atlas drawings. They also expect you to recognize the same framework on **real X-rays**, especially broad landmarks such as the **mandible, clavicle, humerus, ribs, sacrum, pelvic bones, and femur**. That matters because fracture, dislocation, and joint-alignment questions usually start from imaging rather than a cadaveric view.
