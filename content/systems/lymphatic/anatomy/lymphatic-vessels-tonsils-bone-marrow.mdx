This page covers the structures the lecture deck treats as essential but that are easy to lose when lymphatic content gets reduced to only nodes and spleen: **lymphatic vessels, tonsils, and bone marrow**.

## Lymphatic vessels

<Figure name="lymphatic/lymph-vessels-slide" caption="The vessel network, with the right lymphatic duct shown entering the vein at the root of the neck. The circular inset is the part to study: blind-ended lymphatic capillaries lying among the blood capillaries, taking up interstitial fluid the venules could not reabsorb." />

Three features define them:

- **Blind-ended beginnings.** A lymphatic capillary starts as a closed-off tube in the tissue - it has no arterial end feeding it. Its endothelial cells overlap like roof shingles to form **mini-valves**: rising interstitial pressure pushes the flaps inward and fluid enters, but pressure inside the vessel presses them shut so nothing leaks back out. **Anchoring filaments** tether the cells to surrounding collagen, so swelling of the tissue pulls the mini-valves *open* rather than collapsing the vessel. This is also why lymphatic capillaries can admit whole bacteria, cell debris and even tumour cells, which blood capillaries cannot.
- **Valves** in the larger collecting vessels, which is what makes flow one-way.
- **Low pressure and low flow**, with no central pump - which is why skeletal-muscle contraction, breathing and arterial pulsation matter so much for lymph return.

Order of travel: **lymphatic capillaries → collecting vessels → trunks → ducts → veins.** The **lacteals** of the small intestine are specialised lymphatic capillaries that absorb dietary fat, which is why intestinal lymph (**chyle**) is milky.

The **thoracic duct** is the largest lymphatic vessel and returns most of the body's lymph to the venous system; the much smaller **right lymphatic duct** serves only the right head and neck, right arm and right thorax.

## Tonsils

<Figure name="lymphatic/tonsils-slide" caption="Lecture slide showing the palatine tonsils where incoming pharyngeal antigens first meet mucosal immune tissue." />

<Figure name="lymphatic/tonsils-adenoids" caption="Two members of Waldeyer's ring seen through the open mouth: the pharyngeal tonsil (adenoid), outlined behind the soft palate, and the two palatine tonsils flanking the oropharynx - the only ones you can normally inspect. The lingual and tubal tonsils are not visible from this angle." />

The tonsils are exposed directly to the pharynx, so they act like **front-door immune sentries** for inhaled and ingested pathogens. They are part of **MALT** and are often a patient's most visible lymphoid tissue during infection.

Four groups form **Waldeyer's ring** around the entrance to the airway and gut:

| Tonsil | Where | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Pharyngeal** (adenoid) | Roof of the nasopharynx | Single midline mass; enlargement blocks nasal breathing in children |
| **Palatine** | Between the palatoglossal and palatopharyngeal arches | The pair seen on oral exam; the ones removed in tonsillectomy; deep **crypts** trap debris |
| **Lingual** | Posterior third of the tongue | Paired, low-lying |
| **Tubal** | Around the opening of the pharyngotympanic (auditory) tube | Guards the route to the middle ear |

Unlike lymph nodes, tonsils are only **partially encapsulated** and have **no afferent lymphatic vessels** - antigen reaches them straight through the overlying epithelium rather than in arriving lymph.

## Bone marrow

<Figure name="lymphatic/bone-marrow-slide" caption="Every blood cell from one stem cell: the branches lead to red cells, platelets, the granulocytes, monocytes and lymphocytes. The figure itself carries no text - the named version is the lineage chart below." />

Bone marrow is the **production floor** for blood cells:

- **RBCs**
- **WBCs**
- **Platelets**

In immune-system terms, marrow is also a **primary lymphoid organ**: it is where **B cells mature**, and where the precursors that migrate to the thymus to become **T cells** are made. Both lineages come from the same **hematopoietic stem cell**.

## Immune-cell lineages

<Figure name="lymphatic/immune-cells-slide" caption="The named lineage tree. One hematopoietic stem cell divides into a common myeloid progenitor and a common lymphoid progenitor; every cell you will meet in the innate and adaptive chapters hangs off one of those two branches." />

The lineage slide ties the anatomy together:

- **Lymphoid lineage** gives rise to **B cells, T cells, plasma cells, and NK cells**.
- **Myeloid lineage** gives rise to **neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, monocytes/macrophages, mast cells**, and the erythroid-megakaryocyte branch.

That is why bone marrow belongs in the lymphatic block even though it is also part of the skeletal system.
