Gas exchange happens at the alveolus, but oxygen and carbon dioxide still need to **travel through blood** to and from every tissue, and **breathing rate must match** moment-to-moment metabolic demand. This page covers two tightly coupled topics: how blood carries O₂ and CO₂, and how the brainstem decides when to take the next breath.

<Figure name="respiratory/o2-co2-curve" caption="Oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve - sigmoidal because of cooperative O₂ binding to hemoglobin's four heme groups. P50 ≈ 26 mmHg. Right shift (↑ T, ↑ PCO₂, ↑ H⁺/↓pH, ↑ 2,3-BPG) means hemoglobin releases O₂ more readily at the tissue. Left shift hoards O₂." />

## How blood carries oxygen

<Figure name="cardiovascular/o2hb-dissociation" caption="Oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve - sigmoidal cooperative binding. Right shift (high CO2, low pH, high temp, high 2,3-BPG) releases O2 at tissues." />

| Form | Fraction | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Bound to hemoglobin** | ~98.5% | 4 O₂ per Hb tetramer (1 per heme) |
| **Dissolved in plasma** | ~1.5% | 0.003 mL O₂ per dL per mmHg PO₂ - clinically tiny but obeys PaO₂ on ABG |

**O₂ content of blood:** `CaO₂ = (1.34 × Hb × SaO₂) + (0.003 × PaO₂)`

With Hb 15, SaO₂ 100%, PaO₂ 100: ~20.4 mL O₂/dL. The dissolved fraction is 0.3 - tiny.

## The oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve

Hb's affinity for O₂ is **cooperative**: binding one O₂ makes the next three easier. This generates the sigmoidal curve. Three key points:

- **PaO₂ 100 mmHg, SaO₂ 100%** - alveolar level
- **PaO₂ 60 mmHg, SaO₂ ~90%** - the steep slope begins below this; small drops in PaO₂ cause large drops in saturation
- **PaO₂ 40 mmHg, SaO₂ ~75%** - mixed venous (tissue) level

**Shifts of the curve:**

| Right shift (↓ affinity, releases O₂) | Left shift (↑ affinity, hoards O₂) |
|---|---|
| ↑ Temperature | ↓ Temperature |
| ↑ PCO₂ | ↓ PCO₂ |
| ↑ H⁺ (↓ pH) - **Bohr effect** | ↓ H⁺ (↑ pH) |
| ↑ 2,3-BPG (chronic anemia, high altitude) | ↓ 2,3-BPG (stored blood, septic shock) |
| Fetal hemoglobin (HbF) → **left** of adult Hb | |
| Carbon monoxide → severe left + flat curve | |

**Bohr effect**: working tissue is hot, acidic, CO₂-rich → curve shifts right → Hb dumps O₂ where it's needed. Beautiful self-regulation.

<Note title="Anemia, CO poisoning, and the curve">
- **Anemia** lowers O₂ *carrying capacity* (less Hb) but doesn't shift the curve. PaO₂ and SaO₂ remain normal; total O₂ content falls.
- **Carbon monoxide** binds Hb with 240× the affinity of O₂. It does two evil things: occupies sites (less O₂ binding) AND **left-shifts the remaining Hb**, so O₂ won't unload to tissues. SpO₂ pulse oximetry *cannot distinguish* HbO₂ from HbCO → reads falsely normal. Diagnosis requires **co-oximetry**. Treat with 100% O₂; hyperbaric if severe.
- **Methemoglobinemia** - Fe²⁺ oxidized to Fe³⁺ - can't bind O₂. Left shifts the remaining Hb. Pulse ox reads ~85%. Treat with methylene blue.
</Note>

## How blood carries carbon dioxide

CO₂ is 20× more soluble than O₂. Three forms in venous blood:

| Form | Fraction | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)** | ~70% | CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ in red cells, catalyzed by **carbonic anhydrase**. HCO₃⁻ exits via the **Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ antiporter (band 3 / AE1)** → "**chloride shift**." H⁺ buffered by Hb. |
| **Bound to Hb (carbamino)** | ~23% | CO₂ + NH₂ groups of Hb globin chains → carbamino-Hb. **Haldane effect**: deoxygenated Hb binds CO₂ better than oxygenated Hb. |
| **Dissolved in plasma** | ~7% | Obeys PaCO₂ |

**Haldane effect** (the CO₂ counterpart to Bohr): at the lung, oxygenation of Hb releases CO₂ for exhalation; at the tissue, deoxygenation lets Hb pick up more CO₂. The Bohr and Haldane effects are two sides of the same molecular flip.

## Respiratory control - the brainstem rhythm generator

Breathing is automatic but voluntarily overridable. Three control centers in the brainstem:

<Figure name="respiratory/respiratory-centers" caption="Respiratory control centers in the brainstem - medullary dorsal (DRG) and ventral (VRG, including pre-Bötzinger complex, the pacemaker) respiratory groups; pontine pneumotaxic and apneustic centers modulate rate and depth. Inputs from peripheral and central chemoreceptors, lung stretch receptors, and higher CNS." />

| Center | Location | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Dorsal respiratory group (DRG)** | Medulla, nucleus tractus solitarius | Mainly inspiratory; receives chemoreceptor + vagal afferents |
| **Ventral respiratory group (VRG)** | Medulla | Inspiratory + expiratory; includes **pre-Bötzinger complex - the rhythm pacemaker** |
| **Pontine respiratory group (pneumotaxic)** | Upper pons | Limits inspiration → faster, shallower breaths |
| **Apneustic center** | Lower pons | Stimulates inspiration → slower, deeper breaths |

## Chemoreceptors - sensing blood gas tensions

<Figure name="respiratory/respiratory-centers" caption="Chemoreceptor inputs to the respiratory centers - central chemoreceptors (medullary, sense CSF H⁺/PCO2) and peripheral (carotid + aortic bodies, sense PaO2 + PaCO2 + pH)." />

| Receptor | Location | Senses | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Central chemoreceptors** | Ventral surface of medulla | **CSF H⁺ (proxy for arterial PCO₂)** - CO₂ crosses BBB → forms H⁺ in CSF | Most powerful drive for ventilation; tonic and rapid |
| **Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid + aortic bodies)** | Carotid bifurcation; aortic arch | **PaO₂ < 60 mmHg, PaCO₂, H⁺** | Hypoxic ventilatory drive; critical in chronic CO₂ retention |

**Two key points:**
1. Under normal conditions, **PaCO₂ is the dominant driver** of ventilation. Even small rises in PaCO₂ (1-2 mmHg) drive ventilation up dramatically.
2. **PaO₂ only drives ventilation when below ~60 mmHg** (the steep part of the O₂-Hb curve). This is why patients with chronic CO₂ retention (severe COPD) can lose their PaCO₂ drive and rely on hypoxic drive - giving them 100% O₂ can blunt ventilation. (This *concept* matters more than the practical danger, which is overstated; the bigger problem with 100% O₂ in COPD is V/Q mismatch and Haldane reversal.)

## Other reflexes

- **Hering-Breuer inflation reflex** - lung stretch receptors → vagal afferents → inhibit inspiration when lungs over-inflate. Important in infants; weak in adults at normal tidal volumes.
- **J receptors** (juxtacapillary) - in alveolar walls; activated by pulmonary edema or PE → rapid shallow breathing, dyspnea sensation.
- **Irritant receptors** - in airway epithelium; activated by dust, smoke, cold air → cough, bronchoconstriction.
- **Higher CNS** - voluntary breath-hold, speech, laughing, crying override brainstem rhythm.

## Acid-base interplay

<Figure name="urinary/davenport-diagram" caption="Davenport diagram - links respiratory and renal acid-base. Respiratory acidosis (↑ PCO2) renally compensated by ↑ HCO3-; metabolic disturbances respiratorily compensated by ↑/↓ ventilation." />

Blood pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻]/0.03 × PaCO₂) - the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation.

| Disorder | Primary | Compensation | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Respiratory acidosis** | ↑ PaCO₂ | Renal ↑ HCO₃⁻ reabsorption + new HCO₃⁻ | Days |
| **Respiratory alkalosis** | ↓ PaCO₂ | Renal ↓ HCO₃⁻ | Days |
| **Metabolic acidosis** | ↓ HCO₃⁻ | **Hyperventilation** (Kussmaul breathing) - Winter's formula: PaCO₂ = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 ± 2 | Minutes |
| **Metabolic alkalosis** | ↑ HCO₃⁻ | Hypoventilation (limited by hypoxic drive) | Minutes |

The respiratory system compensates for metabolic disturbances within minutes; the kidney compensates for respiratory disturbances in 1-5 days.

## High altitude and acclimatization

<Figure name="cardiovascular/o2hb-dissociation" caption="Altitude acclimatization - acute: hyperventilation (↓ PaCO2 → respiratory alkalosis, left-shift Hb curve); chronic: ↑ EPO → polycythemia, ↑ 2,3-BPG → right-shift Hb curve." />

At 4,000 m, barometric pressure is ~60% of sea level → PiO₂ falls. The body responds in stages:

1. **Acute (seconds-hours)** - peripheral chemoreceptor stim → hyperventilation → respiratory alkalosis. Cardiac output rises.
2. **Days** - kidney excretes HCO₃⁻ to correct alkalosis (so ventilation can rise further without alkalemia limiting it). 2,3-BPG synthesis increases → right shift → better tissue unloading.
3. **Weeks** - erythropoietin rises → hematocrit rises. Capillary density increases. Mitochondrial density in muscle increases.

**Acute mountain sickness (AMS)** - headache, nausea, fatigue at altitude. Severe forms: **HACE** (high-altitude cerebral edema) and **HAPE** (high-altitude pulmonary edema). Treatment: descend; acetazolamide (forces metabolic acidosis → drives respiratory compensation); dexamethasone (HACE); nifedipine (HAPE - reduces pulmonary vasoconstriction).

## Lung volumes recap (functional context)

<Figure name="respiratory/lung-volumes-spirometry" caption="Lung volumes and capacities on spirometry. Tidal volume (TV) ~500 mL; inspiratory reserve (IRV) ~3000 mL; expiratory reserve (ERV) ~1100 mL; residual volume (RV) ~1200 mL (cannot be exhaled, measured indirectly). Capacities: VC = TV + IRV + ERV; FRC = ERV + RV; TLC = all four." />

| Volume / capacity | Definition | Typical adult |
|---|---|---|
| **Tidal volume (TV)** | Quiet breath | ~500 mL |
| **Inspiratory reserve (IRV)** | Additional inhalation above TV | ~3000 mL |
| **Expiratory reserve (ERV)** | Additional exhalation below TV | ~1100 mL |
| **Residual volume (RV)** | Always remains | ~1200 mL |
| **Vital capacity (VC)** | TV + IRV + ERV | ~4600 mL |
| **FRC** | ERV + RV (volume at end-expiration) | ~2300 mL |
| **TLC** | All four | ~5800 mL |
| **FEV₁** | Volume exhaled in first second of forced exhalation | ~80% of FVC |
| **FEV₁/FVC** | Ratio | **< 0.7 = obstructive**; **normal = restrictive** |
