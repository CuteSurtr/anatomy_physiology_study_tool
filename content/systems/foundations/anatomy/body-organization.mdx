The body is organized as **closed cavities (sterile)** that house organs and **open tracts (non-sterile)** that communicate with the outside world. This single distinction explains why drugs go where they go, why bacteria from one site can be benign and from another lethal, and why surgeons reach for very different gowns depending on what they're entering.

## Anatomical position and directional terms

The reference position: standing upright, facing forward, arms at sides, palms forward, feet together.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Anterior / ventral** | Toward the front |
| **Posterior / dorsal** | Toward the back |
| **Superior / cephalic / cranial** | Toward the head |
| **Inferior / caudal** | Toward the feet |
| **Medial** | Toward midline |
| **Lateral** | Away from midline |
| **Proximal** | Closer to point of attachment (limbs) |
| **Distal** | Farther from point of attachment |
| **Superficial** | Closer to surface |
| **Deep** | Farther from surface |
| **Ipsilateral / contralateral** | Same side / opposite side |
| **Supine / prone** | Lying face up / face down |

Planes: **sagittal** (left-right halves), **midsagittal** (exact midline), **coronal/frontal** (front-back halves), **transverse/axial** (top-bottom halves; the plane of CT/MRI slices).

## Open vs closed body cavities - the core principle

The body's cavities split into two clinical worlds:

### Closed cavities (normally sterile)

Sealed, fluid-filled spaces surrounded by membranes. Two big groupings:

- **Dorsal cavity**
  - **Cranial cavity** (skull) - houses the brain
  - **Spinal/vertebral cavity** - houses the spinal cord
- **Ventral cavity** (separated by the diaphragm)
  - **Thoracic cavity** - contains two pleural cavities + one pericardial cavity
  - **Abdominopelvic cavity** - abdominal cavity above pelvic brim, pelvic cavity below

### Open tracts (normally non-sterile)

Communicate with the outside world, harbor commensal flora:
- **Gastrointestinal tract** (mouth → anus)
- **Respiratory tract** (nose → alveoli)
- **Genitourinary tract** (urethra → bladder; vagina → uterus → tubes)

This distinction drives drug administration choices:

| Site of administration | Closed/open | Sterile technique? |
|---|---|---|
| Intrathecal (CSF, subarachnoid space) | Closed | **Strict sterile** - infection here is meningitis |
| Intrapleural (pleural sac) | Closed | Sterile |
| Intraperitoneal | Closed | Sterile |
| Intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, intradermal | Closed (vasculature/tissues) | Sterile |
| Topical / transdermal | Open (skin surface) | Clean, not sterile |
| Oral (PO), nasogastric (NG), rectal (PR) | Open (GI tract) | Clean |
| Inhaled, intranasal | Open (respiratory) | Clean |
| Sublingual (SL), buccal (BUC) | Open | Clean |

<Clinical>
The clinical question - "**should I give antibiotics for a positive culture**" - depends entirely on which cavity the sample came from. Sputum gram-positive cocci: probably normal oropharyngeal flora; treat only with clinical context. Urine GPC: usually contamination from skin or vagina. **Peritoneal fluid GPC**: cavity is sterile - this is **always pathological**. Stool gram-negative rods: normal commensal flora.
</Clinical>

## The four major serous membranes - same architecture, different cavities

Every closed cavity is lined by a serous membrane with the **same two-layer architecture**: a **parietal** layer lining the cavity wall, a **visceral** layer covering the organ inside, and a thin **serous-fluid-filled potential space** between. The membranes are connected at points of organ entry (reflections) so the two layers are continuous.

| Membrane | Cavity | Organ inside | Outer layer (parietal) | Inner layer (visceral) | Special features |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Meninges** | Cranial + spinal (intrathecal space) | Brain + spinal cord | Dura mater (with periosteal + meningeal sublayers in skull only) | Pia mater (adherent to brain), arachnoid mater in between | CSF in subarachnoid space; dural venous sinuses |
| **Pleura** | Pleural (×2, one per lung) | Lung | Parietal pleura (lines chest wall, mediastinum, diaphragm) | Visceral pleura (adherent to lung) | Pleural fluid ~10 mL; pleurisy, effusion, pneumothorax |
| **Peritoneum** | Abdominopelvic | GI organs, accessory organs | Parietal peritoneum (lines wall) | Visceral peritoneum (covers organs) | Peritoneal fluid; mesenteries are folds; ascites in cirrhosis |
| **Pericardium** | Pericardial | Heart | Parietal pericardium + extra **fibrous pericardium** (unique to heart) | Visceral pericardium (epicardium) | Pericardial fluid 15–50 mL; tamponade if rapid accumulation |

The pericardium is unique in having a **third (outer) fibrous** layer that anchors the heart and limits acute distension - which is why a rapid 100 mL effusion can tamponade but a slowly accumulating 500 mL can be tolerated.

## Meninges - the SCALP and beyond

The dorsal cavity walls (skull and vertebrae) are wrapped by layered tissues. The scalp itself, before you even get to bone, has its own five-layer architecture:

<Mnemonic>
**SCALP** - Skin, Connective tissue (dense), Aponeurosis (galea aponeurotica), Loose areolar tissue ("danger zone" - emissary veins), Pericranium (periosteum of skull). Infection in the loose areolar layer can spread across the entire calvarium and into dural venous sinuses via emissary veins.
</Mnemonic>

Then bone, then meninges:

| Layer | Function |
|---|---|
| **Dura mater** | Tough fibrous outer membrane. In the skull it has two sublayers (periosteal + meningeal) that split to enclose **dural venous sinuses**. Inward folds form falx cerebri, tentorium cerebelli, falx cerebelli, diaphragma sellae |
| **Arachnoid mater** | Thin avascular intermediate layer. **Subarachnoid space** between arachnoid and pia contains CSF |
| **Pia mater** | Delicate vascular innermost layer, adherent to brain surface and dipping into every sulcus |

**Three potential/actual spaces** matter clinically:
- **Epidural** (between bone and dura) - site of arterial bleeds from middle meningeal artery (biconvex on CT)
- **Subdural** (between dura and arachnoid) - site of venous bleeds from bridging veins (crescent-shaped on CT)
- **Subarachnoid** (between arachnoid and pia) - CSF lives here; site of arterial aneurysm rupture (thunderclap headache)

## Ventral cavity organization

### Thoracic cavity

Three sub-cavities, each fully enclosed by its own serous membrane:

- **Right pleural cavity** (right lung)
- **Left pleural cavity** (left lung)
- **Pericardial cavity** (heart)

Between them lies the **mediastinum** - a midline compartment containing heart, great vessels, trachea, esophagus, thymus, lymph nodes, and the phrenic + vagus nerves.

### Abdominopelvic cavity

Divided by an imaginary plane at the pelvic brim:
- **Abdominal cavity** above (most GI organs, kidneys, adrenals)
- **Pelvic cavity** below (bladder, rectum, reproductive organs)

The peritoneum lines the abdominopelvic cavity. Organs are classified by their relationship to it:
- **Intraperitoneal** - fully covered by visceral peritoneum, suspended by mesentery (stomach, jejunum, ileum, transverse + sigmoid colon, liver, spleen, gallbladder)
- **Retroperitoneal** - behind the parietal peritoneum (kidneys, adrenals, ureters, duodenum 2–4, ascending + descending colon, pancreas except tail, aorta, IVC)
- **Infraperitoneal / subperitoneal** - below the peritoneal reflection in the pelvis (lower rectum, urinary bladder, distal ureters, pelvic reproductive organs)

<Mnemonic>
**SAD PUCKER** for retroperitoneal organs: **S**uprarenals (adrenals), **A**orta + IVC, **D**uodenum (parts 2–4), **P**ancreas (except tail), **U**reters, **C**olon (ascending + descending), **K**idneys, **E**sophagus (lower thoracic), **R**ectum (mid).
</Mnemonic>

## Embryology in two minutes

The trilaminar embryo (week 3) gives rise to all adult tissues:

| Germ layer | Major derivatives |
|---|---|
| **Ectoderm** | Epidermis, hair, nails; **all** nervous tissue (CNS + PNS); adrenal medulla (neural crest); melanocytes; lens, retina; tooth enamel |
| **Mesoderm** | Muscles (skeletal, cardiac, smooth), bone, cartilage, dermis, blood, blood vessels, heart, kidneys, gonads, adrenal cortex, spleen, serous membranes |
| **Endoderm** | Lining of GI tract, respiratory tract, GU tract; liver, pancreas, thyroid, parathyroids; thymus; auditory tube + middle ear epithelium |

<Note title="Why this matters clinically">
A "retroperitoneal" organ wasn't initially built there - it started intraperitoneal and was pushed back during development (secondary retroperitoneal) or arose retroperitoneal from the start (primary retroperitoneal: kidneys, adrenals, ureters). The **mesentery** is therefore not just a passive drape - it's the developmental record of how each organ migrated.
</Note>

## Every body cavity structure - function reference

| Structure | What it does | Why crucial |
|---|---|---|
| **Cranial cavity** | Houses brain; bone protects against impact | Closed, sterile; infection = meningitis |
| **Spinal cavity** | Houses spinal cord within vertebral column | Continuous with cranial cavity via foramen magnum |
| **CSF (cerebrospinal fluid)** | Cushions CNS, removes waste, ~150 mL total volume | LP samples from L3–L4 or below |
| **Dura mater (cranial)** | Two-layered tough outer covering | Enclosed venous sinuses between layers |
| **Dural folds (falx cerebri, tentorium)** | Compartmentalize brain | Sites of herniation when ICP rises |
| **Arachnoid mater** | Avascular middle layer | Bridging veins cross here → subdural bleed risk |
| **Pia mater** | Adherent inner layer | Carries surface vessels into brain |
| **Subarachnoid space** | CSF compartment | Site of SAH (arterial) |
| **Thoracic cavity** | Contains lungs + heart + mediastinum | Pressure changes drive ventilation |
| **Mediastinum** | Midline compartment in thorax | Houses heart, esophagus, trachea, thymus, great vessels |
| **Pleural cavity (×2)** | Potential space around each lung | Pneumothorax if breached; effusion if fluid accumulates |
| **Pericardial cavity** | Potential space around heart | Tamponade if rapid fluid accumulation |
| **Diaphragm** | Domed musculotendinous floor of thorax | Phrenic-innervated (C3–C5); primary respiratory muscle |
| **Abdominal cavity** | Above pelvic brim | Houses most GI + paired organs |
| **Pelvic cavity** | Below pelvic brim | Reproductive, bladder, rectum |
| **Peritoneal cavity** | Potential space lined by peritoneum | Ascites accumulates here in liver failure |
| **Visceral peritoneum** | Covers intraperitoneal organs | Insensitive to cutting (visceral pain only) |
| **Parietal peritoneum** | Lines abdominal wall | Somatic innervation → localized pain (rebound, guarding) |
| **Mesentery** | Double fold of peritoneum connecting organs to wall | Conduit for vessels, nerves, lymphatics |

## High-yield exam patterns

<HighYield exam="nclex">
**NCLEX clinical correlates:**
- **Sterile vs clean technique** is decided by which cavity you're entering. Intrathecal injection, central line, peritoneal dialysis catheter, lumbar puncture, chest tube → strict sterile.
- **Anatomical position language** is non-negotiable for documentation: "wound on the **lateral** aspect of the **distal** right thigh" is unambiguous; "outside back of the leg" isn't.
- **Lumbar puncture position**: lateral decubitus, fetal/curled, below L3–L4 to avoid the conus medullaris.
- **Cardiac tamponade** is rapid pericardial filling - Beck's triad (hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds). Pericardiocentesis is emergent.
</HighYield>

<HighYield exam="step1">
**Step 1 patterns:**
- **Membrane parietal vs visceral**: parietal innervated somatically → sharp localized pain. Visceral innervated autonomically → vague, poorly-localized, often referred pain.
- **Referred pain examples**: diaphragmatic irritation → **C3–C5 shoulder pain** via phrenic nerve; gallbladder → right shoulder; heart → left arm/jaw.
- **Sterile site list to memorize**: subarachnoid space, pleural cavity, peritoneal cavity, pericardial cavity, joint cavities, blood, urinary tract above the bladder, uterus.
- **Foramen ovale** (heart, not skull) - interatrial septum, closes at birth, leaves fossa ovalis; PFO in ~25% adults.
</HighYield>

<HighYield exam="pharm">
**Drug-administration routes by cavity:**
- **Intrathecal** (baclofen for spasticity, methotrexate for CNS leukemia, anesthetics) - bypasses BBB; minute doses; lethal if confused with IV dose.
- **Intrapleural** (alteplase for loculated effusion, tetracycline for pleurodesis) - local action.
- **Intraperitoneal** (peritoneal dialysis, intraperitoneal chemo for ovarian Ca with mitomycin) - large surface for absorption.
- **Topical / transdermal** - slowest systemic absorption; controlled release.
- **Inhaled** - large alveolar surface, rapid onset, avoids first-pass.
- **Sublingual / buccal** - bypasses first-pass; useful for nitroglycerin in MI.
</HighYield>
