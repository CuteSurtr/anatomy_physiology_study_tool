The kidney's job is not just to make urine - it is to defend the **volume**, **tonicity**, and **electrolyte composition** of the body's fluid compartments. Three hormones do most of the heavy lifting: **ADH** (water), **aldosterone** (sodium and potassium), and **PTH/calcitriol** (calcium and phosphate). Add the **renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS)** as the central regulator of volume status.

<Figure name="cardiovascular/raas" caption="The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. Renin from JG cells cleaves angiotensinogen to angiotensin I; ACE in lung capillaries converts AT-I to AT-II. AT-II triggers vasoconstriction, aldosterone release, ADH release, thirst, and proximal Na⁺/H₂O reabsorption. Aldosterone drives Na⁺ retention and K⁺/H⁺ excretion in the collecting duct." />

## Fluid compartments - the volumes you must know

<Figure name="urinary/water-balance" caption="Daily water balance - inputs (drinking ~1.5 L + food water ~0.7 L + metabolic water ~0.3 L) ≈ outputs (urine ~1.5 L + feces ~0.1 L + insensible loss via skin/lungs ~0.9 L). Maintenance ~2.5 L/day in adults." />

<Figure name="urinary/body-fluid-compartments" caption="Body fluid compartments - total body water (~60% of body weight) split into intracellular (2/3) and extracellular (1/3, made of interstitial + plasma). Na⁺ dominates ECF; K⁺ dominates ICF (Wikimedia)." />

Total body water ≈ **60% of body weight** (a 70 kg adult holds ~42 L).

| Compartment | Fraction of TBW | Volume (70 kg) | Major cation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intracellular fluid (ICF)** | 2/3 (~40%) | ~28 L | K⁺ |
| **Extracellular fluid (ECF)** | 1/3 (~20%) | ~14 L | Na⁺ |
| - Interstitial fluid | 3/4 of ECF | ~10.5 L | |
| - Plasma | 1/4 of ECF | ~3.5 L | |

The cell membrane is freely permeable to **water** but tightly regulates **ions**. So:
- **Tonicity (osmolality)** is the same in all compartments at steady state (~285 mOsm/kg) - water shifts until they are.
- **Sodium** dominates ECF tonicity (~140 mEq/L). Adding Na⁺ to the body without water → ECF tonicity rises → cells shrink.
- **Potassium** dominates ICF tonicity (~140 mEq/L inside the cell; ~4 mEq/L outside).

## The two regulated variables - volume and tonicity

<Figure name="urinary/body-fluid-compartments" caption="Body fluid compartments — volume regulated by RAAS + sympathetic; tonicity regulated independently by ADH. Cells shrink/swell depending on extracellular osmolality." />

The kidney monitors **two different things** with **two different sensors and two different hormones**:

| Variable | Sensor | Effector | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Plasma osmolality (tonicity)** | Hypothalamic osmoreceptors | ADH (vasopressin) | Free water reabsorption in collecting duct |
| **Effective circulating volume** | Carotid + aortic baroreceptors; JG cells | RAAS + ADH + sympathetic | Na⁺ + water retention; vasoconstriction |

Crucially, the body **prioritizes volume over tonicity** when both are off. A hypovolemic patient will retain water *even at the cost of hyponatremia* - which is why severe vomiting, diarrhea, or CHF can cause low Na⁺.

## ADH (antidiuretic hormone / vasopressin) - the water hormone

<Figure name="urinary/nephron-schematic" caption="Nephron + ADH action site. ADH binds V2 receptor on collecting duct principal cells → cAMP → aquaporin-2 insertion → water reabsorption down medullary gradient." />

**Made in** the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus. **Stored in** the posterior pituitary. **Released in response to:**

- **↑ Plasma osmolality** (>285 mOsm/kg) - via osmoreceptors. Most sensitive trigger. A 1% change in osmolality drives ADH release.
- **↓ Effective circulating volume** (>10% drop) - via baroreceptors. Less sensitive but more potent - massive ADH release in hemorrhage / hypovolemia.
- Other - pain, nausea (very potent), stress, opioids, nicotine.

**Mechanism:** binds **V₂ receptors** on principal cells of the collecting duct → Gs/cAMP → **aquaporin-2 channels** insert into the apical membrane → water reabsorption down medullary osmotic gradient. Also binds **V₁ receptors** on vascular smooth muscle → vasoconstriction (hence the name "vasopressin").

**Disorders:**

| Disorder | ADH | Plasma osmolality | Urine osmolality | Plasma Na⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SIADH** | ↑ inappropriately | Low (dilute) | High (concentrated) | Low |
| **Central DI** | ↓ (pituitary problem) | High | Low (dilute) | High |
| **Nephrogenic DI** | ↑ (or normal) but resistance at V₂R | High | Low (dilute) | High |
| **Primary polydipsia** | Appropriately ↓ | Low | Low | Normal or low |

<Clinical>
**Distinguishing central from nephrogenic DI:** give desmopressin (DDAVP, a V₂R agonist). Urine osmolality rises sharply → central. No response → nephrogenic.

**Treatments:**
- **Central DI** → desmopressin nasal spray or oral
- **Nephrogenic DI** → low-salt diet + **thiazides** (paradoxical antidiuretic effect via volume depletion and ↑ proximal reabsorption); amiloride for lithium-induced
- **SIADH** → fluid restriction; **tolvaptan** (V₂ antagonist) if severe; demeclocycline (induces nephrogenic DI - historical); rarely, hypertonic saline (slowly!) for severe symptomatic hyponatremia
</Clinical>

## Aldosterone - the sodium hormone

<Figure name="urinary/raas-aldosterone-openstax" caption="Renin-Aldosterone-Angiotensin axis focused on aldosterone. ATII → adrenal zona glomerulosa → aldosterone → collecting duct → ↑ ENaC + ROMK + Na/K-ATPase → Na+ retention + K+/H+ excretion." />

**Made in** the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex. **Released in response to:**

1. **Angiotensin II** (the dominant trigger) - via the RAAS
2. **Hyperkalemia** - direct stimulation of zona glomerulosa cells
3. **ACTH** - weak, mostly permissive

**Mechanism:** lipid-soluble; enters principal cells → binds mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) → translocates to nucleus → upregulates ENaC, ROMK, Na/K-ATPase. Net effect:
- **Na⁺ reabsorption** in collecting duct (with water following)
- **K⁺ secretion** into urine
- **H⁺ secretion** into urine (via α-intercalated cell H⁺-ATPase)

**Aldosterone escape**: in primary hyperaldosteronism, after several days of Na⁺ retention, ANP/BNP rise and pressure natriuresis kicks in → Na⁺ excretion returns to normal. Hypertension and **hypokalemia persist** because K⁺ secretion does not escape.

## RAAS - the volume regulator

<Figure name="urinary/raas-openstax" caption="Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone pathway (OpenStax). JG cells release renin → cleaves angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → ACE → angiotensin II → vasoconstriction + aldosterone + ADH + thirst." />

The end-to-end loop:

1. **Renin** is released from juxtaglomerular (JG) cells of the afferent arteriole in response to:
   - **↓ afferent arteriolar pressure** (baroreceptor)
   - **↑ sympathetic activity** (β1 receptors)
   - **↓ Na⁺ at the macula densa** (NaCl sensing)
2. Renin cleaves circulating **angiotensinogen** (from liver) → **angiotensin I**.
3. **ACE** (mostly pulmonary endothelium) converts AT-I → **angiotensin II**.
4. **Angiotensin II** has 5 actions:
   - Systemic vasoconstriction (↑ SVR)
   - Adrenal cortex → aldosterone release
   - Posterior pituitary → ADH release
   - Hypothalamus → thirst
   - Proximal tubule → ↑ Na⁺/H₂O reabsorption
   - PCT efferent arteriole > afferent arteriole vasoconstriction → ↑ GFR (preserves filtration despite low renal blood flow)

## Sodium balance - the volume currency

<Figure name="urinary/nephron-schematic" caption="Nephron schematic with diuretic targets - PCT (acetazolamide, SGLT2-i), thick ascending limb (loop diuretics block NKCC2), DCT (thiazides block NCC), collecting duct (amiloride blocks ENaC; spironolactone blocks MR)." />

<Figure name="urinary/renal-blood-flow" caption="Renal blood flow through the nephron - afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries / vasa recta → renal vein. ATII constricts efferent > afferent, preserving GFR when RBF falls." />

Total body Na⁺ determines ECF volume. The kidney can excrete from &lt;1 mEq to &gt;600 mEq per day. Reabsorption by segment:

| Segment | % Na⁺ reabsorbed | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **PCT** | 67% | Na/H exchange, SGLT, Na-amino acid cotransport |
| **Thick ascending limb of Henle** | 25% | NKCC2 (target of loop diuretics) |
| **Distal convoluted tubule** | 5% | NCC (target of thiazides) |
| **Collecting duct (principal cell)** | 3% | ENaC (target of amiloride, spironolactone) |
| **Urine** | ~0.5% | |

**Diuretic targets** follow this map:
- **Acetazolamide** → PCT carbonic anhydrase
- **Mannitol** → osmotic, mainly PCT + thin descending limb
- **Loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide, torsemide)** → NKCC2 in thick ascending limb. Most powerful. Also blunt the medullary gradient.
- **Thiazides** → NCC in DCT. Cause hyponatremia (most common drug cause); hypokalemia, hyperuricemia, hypercalcemia, hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia. ("Hyper-GLUC")
- **K⁺-sparing - amiloride, triamterene** → block ENaC
- **K⁺-sparing - spironolactone, eplerenone** → block MR (aldosterone receptor)
- **SGLT2 inhibitors** → block PCT glucose reabsorption → osmotic diuresis + natriuresis. Cardiorenal mortality benefit.

## Potassium balance

<Figure name="urinary/nephron-schematic" caption="Renal K+ handling - principal cells secrete K+ via apical ROMK channels, driven by Na+ reabsorption + aldosterone. Hyperkalemia → peaked T waves on ECG; hypokalemia → U waves." />

| State | Plasma K⁺ | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypokalemia** (&lt; 3.5) | | Arrhythmia (esp. with digoxin), muscle weakness, ileus, U waves on ECG |
| **Hyperkalemia** (&gt; 5.0) | | Peaked T waves → widened QRS → sine wave → cardiac arrest |

Two systems share regulation:
- **Internal balance** - shifting K⁺ between ICF and ECF. Insulin and β₂ agonists drive K⁺ *into* cells. Acidosis, β-blockers, succinylcholine, cell lysis push K⁺ *out*.
- **External balance** - kidney excretion via aldosterone-driven ROMK in collecting duct principal cells.

**Treating acute hyperkalemia:**
1. **Stabilize membrane** - IV calcium gluconate (does not lower K⁺; protects myocardium)
2. **Shift K⁺ into cells** - insulin + glucose; β₂ agonist (albuterol); bicarbonate if acidemic
3. **Remove K⁺ from body** - loop diuretics (if volume tolerant), potassium binders (patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate, kayexalate), dialysis if needed

## Calcium and phosphate

Tightly regulated by **PTH** and **calcitriol** (active vitamin D). PTH effects:
- ↑ bone resorption → releases Ca²⁺ + PO₄
- ↑ distal tubule Ca²⁺ reabsorption (TRPV5)
- ↓ proximal tubule PO₄ reabsorption → phosphaturia
- ↑ renal 1α-hydroxylase → ↑ calcitriol → ↑ gut Ca²⁺ + PO₄ absorption

Net effect of PTH: ↑ serum Ca²⁺, ↓ serum PO₄. (See **skeletal/physiology/calcium-vitamin-d-pth** for the full axis.)

## ANP and BNP - the volume-unloading hormones

- **Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)** - from atrial myocytes when stretched (high preload)
- **B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP)** - from ventricles when stressed (heart failure)

**Effects:**
- Renal afferent arteriolar **dilation** + efferent constriction → ↑ GFR
- Inhibit Na⁺ reabsorption in collecting duct
- Inhibit renin, aldosterone, ADH

BNP is the labordiagnostic marker for **heart failure** (often > 400 pg/mL when symptomatic).

## Hyponatremia and hypernatremia - the syndromes

| State | ECF volume | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypovolemic hyponatremia** | Low | GI losses, diuretics, third spacing - body retains water to preserve volume |
| **Euvolemic hyponatremia** | Normal | SIADH (most common), psychogenic polydipsia, hypothyroidism, glucocorticoid deficiency |
| **Hypervolemic hyponatremia** | High | CHF, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome - effective volume is low even though total is high |
| **Hypovolemic hypernatremia** | Low | Inadequate water intake, sweating, osmotic diuresis |
| **Euvolemic hypernatremia** | Normal | DI, insensible loss |
| **Hypervolemic hypernatremia** | High | Iatrogenic (hypertonic saline), hyperaldosteronism |

<HighYield exam="step1">
**Rate of sodium correction matters:**
- Chronic hyponatremia (> 48 h) - correct slowly (≤ 8 mEq/L per 24 h) - faster causes **central pontine myelinolysis (osmotic demyelination syndrome)**.
- Chronic hypernatremia - lower slowly (≤ 10 mEq/L per 24 h) - faster causes **cerebral edema**.
- Acute hyponatremia (&lt; 48 h, severe symptoms) - can correct faster with hypertonic saline (1.5-2 mL/kg of 3% boluses).
</HighYield>
