A neuronal action potential is a **rapid, all-or-none reversal of membrane potential** propagating along an axon. Before it can be described, the **resting membrane potential** it starts from has to be accounted for.

<Figure name="nervous/neuron" caption="Multipolar neuron - dendrites receive, soma integrates, axon propagates the action potential to terminal." />

## Resting membrane potential - where −70 mV comes from

The inside of a resting neuron sits about **−70 mV** relative to the outside. Three facts produce that number:

1. **Unequal ion distribution.** The **Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase** pumps **3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in**, using ATP. At steady state K⁺ is high inside (~140 mM) and low outside (~5 mM); Na⁺ is high outside (~145 mM) and low inside (~12 mM). Large intracellular anions (proteins, phosphates) cannot cross the membrane at all.
2. **At rest the membrane is far more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺**, because **K⁺ leak channels** are open while voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are shut. K⁺ leaves down its concentration gradient until the negative charge left behind pulls it back; that balance point - the **equilibrium potential for K⁺ (E_K)** - is about **−90 mV**.
3. **A small steady Na⁺ leak inward** (E_Na ≈ +60 mV) holds the membrane slightly positive of E_K. The resting potential therefore settles near **−70 mV**, close to E_K because K⁺ permeability dominates.

The pump's *direct* electrogenic contribution is only a few mV; its real job is maintaining the gradients that the leak channels convert into a voltage. Poisoning the pump does not abolish the resting potential instantly - it runs down over minutes as the gradients dissipate.

<Note title="Vocabulary used from here on">
**Depolarization** = the membrane potential becomes *less* negative (moves toward 0 mV). **Hyperpolarization** = it becomes *more* negative. **Graded potentials** are small, decremental voltage changes produced by ligand-gated channels on dendrites and soma; only if they sum to **threshold (~−55 mV)** at the axon hillock does an action potential fire.
</Note>

## Phases

<Figure name="nervous/action-potential" caption="The action potential plotted against time. Subthreshold stimuli produce failed initiations; once threshold (−55 mV) is crossed the membrane depolarizes to a positive peak, repolarizes, undershoots below rest, then returns to −70 mV." />

1. **Resting** (−70 mV): voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels closed; K⁺ leak dominates.
2. **Depolarization to threshold** (~−55 mV): graded summation from dendrites at the axon hillock.
3. **Rising phase**: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open → Na⁺ rushes in → membrane swings to ~+30 mV.
4. **Peak**: Na⁺ channels inactivate; voltage-gated K⁺ channels open.
5. **Repolarization**: K⁺ flows out, membrane returns toward rest.
6. **Hyperpolarization (undershoot)**: K⁺ channels close slowly - membrane briefly more negative than rest.
7. **Return to rest**: K⁺ channels close; Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase maintains gradients long-term.

## Refractory periods

- **Absolute refractory** (during phases 3–5): Na⁺ channels are inactivated - no new AP can fire, regardless of stimulus strength. This is what keeps APs unidirectional.
- **Relative refractory** (phase 6): a stronger-than-normal stimulus can trigger another AP.

## Propagation

<Figure name="nervous/axon-propagation" caption="Saltatory conduction along a myelinated axon. The AP regenerates only at nodes of Ranvier; internodal regions spread passively. Much faster + more energy-efficient than continuous conduction in unmyelinated axons." />

- **Unmyelinated axons**: continuous propagation; slow (~1 m/s).
- **Myelinated axons**: **saltatory** conduction - APs jump between **nodes of Ranvier** where voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated. Speeds reach ~100 m/s.

<Figure name="nervous/oligodendrocytes" caption="Oligodendrocytes - CNS myelin-forming glia. A single oligodendrocyte wraps several different axon segments at once (PNS Schwann cells, by contrast, wrap one segment each). The target of demyelination in multiple sclerosis." />

<Clinical>
**Multiple sclerosis** is autoimmune demyelination of CNS axons. Loss of myelin → slowed or blocked conduction → variable neurologic deficits depending on plaque location. **Guillain-Barré** is the peripheral analog (post-infectious demyelination of PNS).
</Clinical>

<Note title="Why the AP is all-or-none">
Once threshold is crossed, the Na⁺ influx is positive feedback (depolarization → more Na⁺ channels open → more depolarization). The amplitude doesn't encode stimulus strength - **frequency** does.
</Note>

## Synaptic transmission

When the AP reaches the **presynaptic terminal**:

1. Voltage-gated **Ca²⁺ channels** open → Ca²⁺ enters the terminal.
2. Ca²⁺ triggers **synaptic vesicles** (full of neurotransmitter) to fuse with the membrane (via SNARE proteins).
3. Neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft, binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
4. Postsynaptic effect: excitatory (EPSP, e.g., glutamate at AMPA/NMDA → Na⁺ in) or inhibitory (IPSP, e.g., GABA at GABA-A → Cl⁻ in).
5. Termination: reuptake (most neurotransmitters), enzymatic breakdown (ACh by acetylcholinesterase), or diffusion away.

## Every ion channel and neurotransmitter - function reference

### Voltage-gated channels

| Channel | Function | Where | Drugs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Na⁺ (Nav)** | Rapid depolarization | Throughout neuron, especially nodes of Ranvier | Local anesthetics (lidocaine), Class I antiarrhythmics, phenytoin |
| **K⁺ (Kv)** | Repolarization | Throughout | Class III antiarrhythmics (amiodarone, sotalol) |
| **Ca²⁺ (L-type)** | Sustained depolarization in cardiac/smooth muscle; neurotransmitter release in synapse | Cardiac, smooth muscle, presynaptic terminals | DHP CCBs (amlodipine), non-DHP CCBs (verapamil, diltiazem) |
| **Ca²⁺ (N, P/Q types)** | Presynaptic Ca²⁺ entry | Neurons | Gabapentin (α2δ), ziconotide |
| **Ca²⁺ (T-type)** | Pacemaker, thalamic oscillations | SA node, thalamus | Ethosuximide (absence seizures) |

| NT | Class | Receptors | Function | Drugs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Glutamate** | Amino acid | AMPA, NMDA, kainate (ionotropic); mGluR (metabotropic) | Major excitatory NT | Ketamine, memantine (NMDA antagonists) |
| **GABA** | Amino acid | GABA-A (Cl⁻), GABA-B (G-coupled K⁺) | Major inhibitory NT | Benzos, barbs, propofol potentiate GABA-A |

## High-yield exam patterns

<HighYield exam="nclex">
**NCLEX neuro patient teaching:**
- **Multiple sclerosis**: avoid heat (worsens symptoms - Uhthoff phenomenon); rest periods to manage fatigue; teach injection technique for DMT (e.g., interferon-β).
- **Guillain-Barré**: ascending paralysis; monitor respiratory function (VC, NIF) - intubate before failure.
- **Seizure first aid**: protect head, time the seizure, side-lying after; **status epilepticus** &gt;5 min → emergency.
- **Anti-epileptic teaching**: never stop abruptly (status risk); take consistently; monitor levels.
</HighYield>

<HighYield exam="pharm">
**CNS pharm essentials:**
- **Benzodiazepines** (diazepam, lorazepam, midazolam): GABA-A potentiation - ↑ frequency of Cl⁻ opening; reversal = **flumazenil**.
- **Barbiturates**: GABA-A - ↑ duration; lethal in overdose (resp depression).
- **SSRIs**: 4–6 wk for full effect; suicidality warning in young adults; serotonin syndrome if combined with MAOI/triptan.
- **Lithium**: narrow therapeutic window (0.6–1.2 mEq/L); monitor renal, thyroid; dehydration → toxicity.
- **Antipsychotics**: typicals (haloperidol) → EPS, tardive dyskinesia; atypicals → metabolic syndrome (weight, glucose, lipids).
- **Triptans**: contraindicated in CAD, pregnancy.
</HighYield>
