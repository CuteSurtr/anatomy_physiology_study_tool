The special senses share a common architecture: a **receptor cell** transduces a physical stimulus into a graded receptor potential, which modulates neurotransmitter release onto a primary afferent neuron that projects to the brain. The details - the physics of vision and hearing, the chemistry of taste and smell - vary, but the wiring follows recognizable patterns.

## Vision - phototransduction

<Figure name="nervous/retina" caption="Retinal histology - light passes through ganglion cells, bipolar cells, and amacrine/horizontal cells before reaching photoreceptors. ### Rods vs cones

| | Rods | Cones |
|---|---|---|
| **Number** | ~120 million | ~6 million |
| **Pigment** | Rhodopsin (peak 500 nm, blue-green) | 3 opsins - S (blue, 420), M (green, 530), L (red, 560) |
| **Sensitivity** | High (a single photon can trigger) | Lower (needs ~100 photons) |
| **Speed** | Slow | Fast |
| **Convergence** | Many rods per ganglion → high sensitivity, low acuity | Few cones per ganglion (1:1 at fovea) → high acuity |
| **Location** | Peripheral retina (none in fovea) | Densely packed in fovea (none in far periphery) |
| **Function** | Scotopic (night) vision; movement | Photopic (day) vision; color; fine detail |

### The phototransduction cascade

In **darkness**, photoreceptors are *depolarized* (~-40 mV) and continuously releasing **glutamate**. Light **hyperpolarizes** them and *reduces* glutamate release.

1. **Dark state**: high intracellular cGMP keeps cation channels open → steady Na⁺/Ca²⁺ inflow → membrane at -40 mV → continuous glutamate release.
2. **Light**: photon hits rhodopsin → retinal isomerizes (11-cis → all-trans) → activates **transducin** (G protein) → activates **PDE6** → hydrolyzes cGMP → cGMP-gated channels close → Na⁺/Ca²⁺ inflow stops → photoreceptor hyperpolarizes → less glutamate.
3. **Reset**: all-trans retinal regenerates to 11-cis (slow; requires vitamin A in RPE).

### Receptive fields and signal processing

Bipolar cells receive direct input from photoreceptors. Each bipolar cell has a **center-surround receptive field** organized by horizontal cells.

- **ON-center bipolar cells** depolarize when light hits the receptive field center, hyperpolarize when light hits the surround.
- **OFF-center bipolar cells** do the opposite.

This **lateral inhibition** sharpens edges and emphasizes contrast - the basis for visual acuity and the Mach band illusion.

### Pathway from retina to cortex

Photoreceptor → Bipolar cell → **Ganglion cell** (first cell that fires action potentials) → **Optic nerve (CN II)** → **Optic chiasm** (nasal fibers cross, temporal don't) → **Optic tract** → **Lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN, thalamus)** → **Optic radiations** → **Primary visual cortex (V1, calcarine sulcus, occipital lobe)**.

The temporal optic radiation **(Meyer's loop)** carries upper visual field info and loops through the temporal lobe → vulnerable to temporal lobe lesions ("**pie in the sky**" contralateral superior quadrantanopia).

### Common visual field defects

| Defect | Lesion |
|---|---|
| **Monocular blindness** | Optic nerve (CN II) |
| **Bitemporal hemianopsia** | Optic chiasm (pituitary tumor, craniopharyngioma) |
| **Homonymous hemianopsia** | Optic tract / LGN / radiation / V1 of contralateral side |
| **Superior quadrantanopia** ("pie in the sky") | Temporal lobe (Meyer's loop) |
| **Inferior quadrantanopia** ("pie on the floor") | Parietal lobe radiation |
| **Macular sparing** with homonymous hemianopsia | V1 lesion with collateral PCA + MCA supply to macular region |

### Refraction errors and the lens

| Error | Mechanism | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| **Myopia** (nearsighted) | Eye too long or cornea too curved → image focuses in front of retina | Concave (minus) lens |
| **Hyperopia** (farsighted) | Eye too short or cornea too flat → image would focus behind retina | Convex (plus) lens |
| **Astigmatism** | Non-spherical cornea | Cylindrical lens |
| **Presbyopia** | Lens stiffens with age → loss of accommodation | Reading glasses (plus power) |

**Accommodation** = ciliary muscle contracts → zonular fibers relax → lens becomes more spherical → more refractive power → near focus. Loss with age (lens hardens).

## Hearing - mechanotransduction

<Figure name="nervous/ear-anatomy" caption="The ear has three regions: external (pinna + canal), middle (tympanic membrane, ossicles - malleus, incus, stapes), and inner (cochlea + vestibular apparatus). Sound waves vibrate the tympanic membrane → ossicles amplify mechanical pressure ~20× → stapes pushes oval window → fluid wave in cochlear scala vestibuli → bends hair cells on the basilar membrane → ion influx → afferent firing on CN VIII." />

### The cochlea - place coding

The **basilar membrane** is wider and floppier at the **apex** (cochlear tip) and narrower and stiffer at the **base**. Different frequencies cause peak vibration at different positions:

- **High frequencies (~20 kHz)** - peak at base (narrow, stiff)
- **Low frequencies (~20 Hz)** - peak at apex (wide, floppy)

This **tonotopic map** is preserved through the auditory pathway all the way to cortex. Each hair cell signals one preferred frequency.

### Hair cells

**Inner hair cells** (one row, ~3,500 total) are the principal sensors. **Outer hair cells** (three rows, ~12,000) are mechanically active - they shorten and lengthen with sound → amplify basilar membrane motion ~100× → the cochlear amplifier. Damage to outer hair cells (loud noise, ototoxic drugs) causes sensorineural hearing loss.

**Mechanotransduction**: stereocilia bend → tip links pulled → MET channel opens → K⁺ enters from K⁺-rich endolymph → cell depolarizes → glutamate release onto CN VIII afferents.

### Endocochlear potential

The **stria vascularis** pumps K⁺ into endolymph, generating a +80 mV endolymphatic potential that drives the K⁺ influx through MET channels. Disorders of stria vascularis (Ménière disease, autoimmune inner ear) cause hearing loss + vertigo.

### Auditory pathway

Hair cell → CN VIII → **cochlear nucleus** (medulla) → **superior olivary nucleus** (bilateral input here → sound localization via interaural time and intensity differences) → **lateral lemniscus** → **inferior colliculus** (midbrain) → **medial geniculate nucleus** (thalamus) → **primary auditory cortex (A1, Heschl gyrus, superior temporal gyrus)**.

### Conductive vs sensorineural hearing loss

| | Conductive | Sensorineural |
|---|---|---|
| **Lesion** | External or middle ear | Cochlea or CN VIII |
| **Weber test** (tuning fork on forehead) | Lateralizes to bad ear | Lateralizes to good ear |
| **Rinne test** (mastoid vs ear canal) | BC > AC in affected ear (abnormal) | AC > BC in affected ear (normal pattern, both diminished) |
| **Examples** | Cerumen, OM, otosclerosis, perforation | Presbycusis, noise exposure, ototoxic drugs (aminoglycosides, cisplatin, loop diuretics, salicylates), Ménière, vestibular schwannoma |

## Vestibular system - balance and head motion

Three **semicircular canals** detect angular acceleration (rotation in each of three planes). **Utricle and saccule** detect linear acceleration and gravity via otoliths.

Vestibular afferents → vestibular nuclei (brainstem) → cerebellum, oculomotor nuclei (**vestibulo-ocular reflex** keeps gaze stable during head movement), spinal cord (postural reflexes), cortex (sense of orientation).

**Disorders:**
- **BPPV** - otolith debris in semicircular canal → brief positional vertigo. Treat with Epley maneuver.
- **Ménière disease** - endolymphatic hydrops → episodic vertigo + hearing loss + tinnitus + ear fullness.
- **Vestibular neuritis** - acute sustained vertigo + nausea; no hearing loss.
- **Vestibular schwannoma** (acoustic neuroma) - CN VIII tumor → unilateral sensorineural hearing loss + tinnitus + balance issues.

## Taste

Five basic tastes:

| Taste | Receptor | Stimulus |
|---|---|---|
| **Sweet** | T1R2 + T1R3 GPCRs | Sugars, sucralose |
| **Bitter** | T2R family GPCRs | Quinine, caffeine, many plant toxins |
| **Umami** | T1R1 + T1R3 GPCRs | Glutamate, aspartate |
| **Salt** | Na⁺ via ENaC | Na⁺ |
| **Sour** | H⁺ via PKD2L1 / proton channels | Acids |

Taste buds on the tongue contain **type I (support), II (taste signal - sweet/bitter/umami), III (sour)** cells. Salty taste is mediated by direct ion entry.

**Afferents:**
- Anterior 2/3 of tongue → CN VII (chorda tympani)
- Posterior 1/3 → CN IX (glossopharyngeal)
- Epiglottis → CN X (vagus)

All converge on the **nucleus tractus solitarius** in the medulla → thalamus → gustatory cortex (insula + frontal operculum).

## Smell - the most direct pathway to cortex

**Olfactory receptors** in the olfactory epithelium are GPCRs - ~400 functional types in humans, each cell expressing one. Each odor activates a unique combination → combinatorial coding allows discrimination of ~10⁴ odors.

Olfactory receptor neuron → **olfactory bulb** (via cribriform plate of ethmoid) → **olfactory tract** → directly to **primary olfactory cortex** (piriform cortex, amygdala, entorhinal cortex) - **the only sense that does NOT relay through thalamus before reaching cortex**.

**Olfactory pathway is also the only route for prion proteins, polio, and amoebic meningoencephalitis** (Naegleria fowleri) to access the brain.

**Anosmia** (loss of smell):
- Head trauma (shearing of fila olfactoria at cribriform plate)
- Upper respiratory viral infection (including COVID-19)
- Olfactory groove meningioma (Foster-Kennedy syndrome)
- Neurodegenerative disease (Parkinson, Alzheimer - early sign)
- Kallmann syndrome (anosmia + hypogonadotropic hypogonadism)
