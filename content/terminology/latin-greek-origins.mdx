Most medical terminology is built on Latin (anatomy) + Greek (specialties, conditions). Knowing the meaning of these roots is the multiplier that makes terms decode-able.

## Why two languages?

- **Latin** = the language of medieval European medicine + the formal naming of anatomy (Terminologia Anatomica). Anatomic terms: **vena cava**, **musculus**, **corpus**, **ligamentum**.
- **Greek** = the language of Hippocrates + classical medicine; preferred for **disease + procedure** names. Specialty terms: **pneumonia**, **cardiology**, **endoscopy**.

The two languages often have parallel terms for the same thing:

| English | Latin | Greek |
|---|---|---|
| Liver | hepar (rare) | hepar → **hepat/o** |
| Kidney | ren → **ren/o, renal** | nephros → **nephr/o** |
| Bladder | vesica → **vesic/o** | kystis → **cyst/o** |
| Stomach | venter, stomachus | gaster → **gastr/o** |
| Skin | cutis → **cutane/o** | derma → **dermat/o** |
| Eye | oculus → **ocul/o** | ophthalmos → **ophthalm/o** |
| Heart | cor → **cor-, coron-** | kardia → **cardi/o** |
| Bone | os → **oss-** | osteon → **oste/o** |
| Mouth | os → **or-** | stoma → **stomat/o** |
| Muscle | musculus → **muscul/o** | mys → **my/o** |
| Lung | pulmo → **pulmon/o** | pneumon → **pneumon/o** |
| Womb | uterus → **uter/o** | hystera → **hyster/o**; metra → **metr/o** |

Why both survive: anatomic naming favors Latin (Terminologia Anatomica); clinical/specialty terms favor Greek (logical for "study of," "-itis," "-ectomy").

## Pronunciation tips

### Classical vs Anglicized

| Word | Classical | Anglicized |
|---|---|---|
| **Cervix** | KER-vix | SER-vix |
| **Cilia** | KEE-lee-a | SIL-ee-a |
| **Psychology** | psyche- starts with hard P | sigh-KOL-uh-jee |
| **Pneumonia** | hard P | nu-MOH-nee-ah (silent P) |
| **Phthisis** | f-thy-sis | TYE-sis |
| **Ptosis** | p-toh-sis | TOH-sis |
| **Mnemonic** | m-nem-on-ik | ni-MON-ik |
| **Xeromata** | ze-ROM-uh-tuh | ZEE-roh-MAH-tuh |

In standard American medical usage, anglicized pronunciation dominates - silent initial consonants in **ps**ychology, **pn**eumonia, **pt**erygium.

### "ch" before -e, -i

Greek loan: pronounced "k": **chronic** (KRON-ik), **cholera** (KOL-er-uh), **chiasm** (KYE-azm), **chyme** (KIME).
English: pronounced "ch": **chimney**, **change**.

### "ph" = "f"

Always: **ph**armacy, **ph**arynx, **ph**ase, em**ph**ysema.

### Latin "-ae"

Plural ending; pronounced "ee" or "ay" (varies regionally):
**vertebrae** (ver-tuh-BRAY or VER-tuh-bray), **bursae** (BUR-say or BUR-see).

### Latin/Greek with silent letters

- **Pneumonia, ptosis, psoas, ptyalin** - silent first letter.
- **Phthisis** - silent or barely-pronounced "ph."

## Common etymological "aha" moments

- **Cancer / Carcinoma** = "crab" (karkinos) - because tumors with veins radiating outward looked like crabs.
- **Coronary** = "crown" - the coronary arteries form a crown around the heart.
- **Atrium** = "entrance hall" in Latin - the entry chamber of the heart.
- **Aorta** = Greek "lifter" - because it's hung from above.
- **Tonsil** = Latin "tonsilla" = anchor stone.
- **Hippocampus** = "horse" + "sea monster" - the brain structure resembles a seahorse.
- **Cerebellum** = "little brain" in Latin.
- **Hypochondrium** = "below the cartilage" - the region under the ribs.
- **Spleen** = Greek "splen" - associated with melancholy in ancient medicine.
- **Pancreas** = "all flesh" in Greek.
- **Insulin** = "of the islands" - insulin comes from islets of Langerhans.
- **Vagina** = Latin "sheath."
- **Penis** = Latin "tail."
- **Diaphragm** = Greek "partition" or "fence."
- **Sciatica** = Greek "ischion" = hip.
- **Sphincter** = Greek "to bind tight."
- **Gangrene** = Greek "gangraina" = decay.
- **Epilepsy** = "to seize on" - epilepsia.
- **Migraine** = Greek "hemikrania" = half head.
- **Quarantine** = Italian "quaranta giorni" = 40 days (plague isolation).
- **Vaccination** = Latin "vacca" = cow (cowpox - Jenner).
- **Doctor** = Latin "to teach."
- **Physician** = Greek "physis" = nature.

## Roots that switched meaning

| Term | Original | Modern |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardia (of stomach)** | Greek "heart-like" | Upper portion of stomach near LES |
| **Cyst** | Greek "bladder" | Any sac (renal cyst, sebaceous cyst) |
| **Cranium** | Greek "skull" | Now broad, includes bony skull + meninges |
| **Sclera** | Greek "hard" | The hard outer layer of the eye |

## The Hippocratic legacy

Greek medical terminology - much from Hippocratic corpus - established **descriptive disease names** centered on body parts + their qualities. Most "-itis," "-emia," "-pathy" suffixes come from this tradition.

Modern descriptive medicine continues: COVID-19 = "corona-virus-disease-2019." Names attempt to be neutral + geographic (not "Spanish flu," "Wuhan virus").

## Why pronunciation matters

In high-stakes verbal communication (handoffs, dictations), correct pronunciation prevents:
- Misidentification of drugs (LASA).
- Misinterpretation of conditions.
- Misunderstanding of body parts.

**When in doubt**: spell it out. "C-as-in-Charlie, h-as-in-hotel..."
