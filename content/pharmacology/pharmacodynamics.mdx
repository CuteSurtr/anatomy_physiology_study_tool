Pharmacodynamics (PD) is **what the drug does to the body**: how it binds to its target, how that produces an effect, and how potency, efficacy, and toxicity scale with dose.

## Receptors and drug-target interactions

Most drugs work by binding to a specific **target macromolecule** - usually a receptor, but also enzymes, transporters, ion channels, or structural proteins.

| Target | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR)** | Heterotrimeric G-protein → second messenger | β-adrenergic (Gs → cAMP), muscarinic M2 (Gi → ↓ cAMP), opioid (Gi), histamine H1 (Gq) |
| **Ion channel (ligand-gated)** | Direct ion flux | Nicotinic ACh receptor, GABA-A, NMDA |
| **Ion channel (voltage-gated)** | Channel-blocking drugs | Local anesthetics (Na+), Ca-channel blockers, antiarrhythmics |
| **Receptor tyrosine kinase** | Autophosphorylation → RAS/MAPK or PI3K | Insulin, EGFR, VEGF, growth factors |
| **Nuclear receptor** | Cytoplasm → nucleus → transcription | Glucocorticoids, thyroid hormone, vitamin D, sex steroids, PPARγ (glitazones) |
| **JAK-STAT receptor** | Cytokine receptors → Janus kinase | Erythropoietin, growth hormone, prolactin, many cytokines |
| **Enzyme** | Direct inhibition or activation | ACE inhibitors, statins, NSAIDs (COX), DOACs (factor Xa, thrombin) |
| **Transporter** | Block reuptake or transport | SSRIs (SERT), SGLT2 (renal glucose), PPIs (H+/K+-ATPase) |

## Dose-response and the four key parameters

A dose-response curve has four important features:

- **Potency (EC50)** = dose producing 50% of maximal effect. Left-shift = more potent. Determined by receptor affinity. Not clinically critical (just means you give a smaller pill).
- **Efficacy (Emax)** = maximal achievable response. Determined by the drug-receptor system. Clinically critical - what you can actually accomplish.
- **Slope** = how rapidly response changes with dose - steep slope means small dose changes cause large effect changes (narrow therapeutic window).
- **Therapeutic index (TI)** = `TD50 / ED50` (or `LD50 / ED50` for animal data). Wide TI = safe; narrow TI = dangerous (digoxin, lithium, warfarin, theophylline, phenytoin).

## Agonists, antagonists, and modulators

| Class | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Full agonist** | Binds + activates receptor maximally | Morphine at μ-opioid receptor; isoproterenol at β |
| **Partial agonist** | Binds + activates submaximally (lower Emax) | Buprenorphine at μ-opioid; pindolol at β; varenicline at α4β2 nicotinic |
| **Inverse agonist** | Binds + produces opposite of intrinsic constitutive activity | Some antihistamines at H1 (decrease constitutive activity) |
| **Competitive antagonist** | Binds same site reversibly; surmountable by ↑ agonist | Naloxone at opioid; atropine at muscarinic |
| **Non-competitive (irreversible) antagonist** | Binds covalently or to allosteric site; ↓ Emax | Phenoxybenzamine at α (irreversible); aspirin at COX |
| **Allosteric modulator** | Binds elsewhere; alters affinity or activity | Benzodiazepines at GABA-A (potentiate); etomidate at GABA-A |
| **Functional antagonist** | Opposes physiologic effect by a different pathway | β-blockers vs α-agonists for hypertension |

**Competitive vs non-competitive on dose-response**: competitive antagonist shifts the agonist curve right (↑ EC50) but preserves Emax. Non-competitive lowers Emax without shifting EC50.

## Tolerance, dependence, tachyphylaxis

- **Tolerance** - need ↑ dose for same effect after repeated exposure. Mechanisms: receptor downregulation (β-agonists), receptor desensitization (nicotinic on continuous succinylcholine), enzyme induction (ethanol), depletion of endogenous mediators (tachyphylaxis with ephedrine).
- **Tachyphylaxis** - rapid tolerance, often after just one or two doses (ephedrine, nitrates if dosed continuously, LSD).
- **Cross-tolerance** - tolerance to one drug produces tolerance to another acting at the same target (alcohol ↔ benzodiazepines ↔ barbiturates - all GABA-A potentiators).
- **Physical dependence** - withdrawal syndrome when drug is stopped (opioids, alcohol, benzodiazepines, β-blockers, clonidine). Distinct from addiction.

## Receptor desensitization and downregulation

Chronic agonist → receptor phosphorylation → β-arrestin binding → internalization (desensitization, minutes). Sustained → ↓ receptor synthesis (downregulation, hours–days). Restoration on agonist withdrawal explains rebound hypertension after clonidine stop, rebound tachycardia after β-blocker withdrawal.

## Spare receptors

For some drugs, maximal response is reached before all receptors are bound - "spare receptors" provide reserve. Implication: a small fraction of irreversible antagonism may not block maximal agonist response until the spare receptor reserve is exhausted. Common with β-adrenergic and acetylcholine receptors.

## Drug-drug interactions

PD interactions are about the **effect**, not the level:

| Type | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Additive** | 1 + 1 = 2 | Two NSAIDs, or NSAID + aspirin (similar mechanism) |
| **Synergistic** | 1 + 1 > 2 | β-lactam + aminoglycoside (cell wall hole → AG entry); ethanol + benzodiazepine (both CNS depressants) |
| **Potentiation** | Drug A alone has no effect, but enhances B | Carbidopa (no antiparkinsonian effect alone) + levodopa |
| **Antagonism** | Drug A opposes B | Naloxone reverses opioid; flumazenil reverses BZD; vitamin K reverses warfarin |

## Therapeutic and toxic index

| Drug | TI | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Digoxin** | Narrow | Toxicity → arrhythmias; monitor levels |
| **Warfarin** | Narrow | Bleeding; monitor INR |
| **Lithium** | Narrow | Toxicity → tremor, ataxia, seizures; monitor level, especially with NSAIDs, diuretics |
| **Theophylline** | Narrow | Toxicity → seizures, arrhythmias |
| **Phenytoin** | Narrow | Zero-order kinetics + saturable metabolism |
| **Aminoglycosides** | Narrow | Nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity |
| **Vancomycin** | Narrow | Nephrotoxicity, "red man" |
| **Levothyroxine** | Narrow | Hyper-/hypothyroid |

## Quantal dose-response and population variability

Beyond the single-subject curve, populations show variability:
- **ED50** = dose producing therapeutic effect in 50% of population.
- **TD50** = dose producing toxicity in 50%.
- **LD50** = dose lethal in 50% (animal data only).
- **TI** = TD50/ED50 (or LD50/ED50). The **certain safety factor** (CSF) = TD1/ED99 is a stricter clinical metric.

## Hill equation (for the curious)

```
E = (Emax × [D]^n) / (EC50^n + [D]^n)
```

n = Hill coefficient = slope steepness; n=1 means one molecule per binding site; n>1 means cooperative binding (hemoglobin/O2 has n≈2.8).

<HighYield variant="boards">
- Distinguish potency from efficacy: morphine is **more potent** than codeine but they have **the same maximum analgesia (efficacy)**. Acetaminophen has less efficacy than morphine no matter the dose.
- A competitive antagonist preserves Emax (more agonist overcomes it). Irreversible antagonist reduces Emax.
- Partial agonists at full receptor occupancy can act as functional antagonists in the presence of a full agonist (buprenorphine in opioid-tolerant patient precipitates withdrawal).
- Diazepam's anxiolytic effect at GABA-A is via allosteric potentiation (increases frequency of channel opening, not directly opening it). Barbiturates increase channel **duration**.
- The therapeutic window narrows in zero-order kinetics: small dose changes can push phenytoin or salicylate from therapeutic to toxic.
</HighYield>
